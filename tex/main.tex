\abstract{%
  Small scientific computing sites run a handful of stateful grid
  services on a handful of machines, administered by one or two people
  for whom cluster operation is one duty among several. Such sites need
  real high availability but cannot absorb the operational surface of a
  general-purpose orchestrator. Our design follows from three
  constraints: a small team argues for the availability layer the
  operating-system vendor supports directly, so we use Pacemaker and
  Corosync rather than a separate control plane; containerised services
  argue for Podman Quadlet, which turns container definitions into
  ordinary systemd units that Pacemaker manages without an adapter; and
  the absence of external shared storage -- no NFS server, no SAN --
  makes DRBD replication between the cluster nodes the means by which a
  service finds its configuration and state on whichever node currently
  runs it. The hard part of this stack is the chain of Pacemaker
  resource, operation and constraint definitions that binds DRBD
  promotion to the start of the dependent containers, because its
  failure modes are silent: several configurations are accepted by every
  tool and are nevertheless wrong. We describe that chain in detail, and
  the tool we built to generate it -- \emph{IPVLAN Container Manager},
  which emits DRBD resource files, Quadlet units, \texttt{pcs} setup and
  teardown scripts, nftables ingress policy and the deploying Ansible
  playbooks, while adding no runtime component to the cluster. We report
  the validation strategy adopted after unit tests were found to accept
  artefacts that the real tools reject, and experience from the
  production three-node deployment of the CMS Tier-2 site
  T2\_KR\_KISTI.
}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

High availability is usually discussed in terms of scale. In practice a
large number of scientific computing sites are constrained by something
else: the number of people available to operate the system. A CMS
Tier-2 centre, a campus analysis facility or a regional data centre
typically runs a small set of services that must not be down for long --
a storage front-end, a compute-element gateway, an information and
accounting publisher -- on three to seven machines, administered by one
or two staff members with other responsibilities.

For that population the dominant cost of an HA deployment is not
hardware but the amount of operational knowledge that must stay resident
in a very small team and remain recoverable after an interruption of
weeks. Kubernetes and comparable platforms~\cite{kubernetes} solve
availability by introducing a control plane that is itself a distributed
system to be kept alive, upgraded on its own cadence and debugged
through its own abstractions; a small team can adopt that, but it then
owns a second operational problem larger than the first. Conversely,
managing containers by hand through systemd units or command-line
invocations is error-prone and verbose, and provides no central place in
which the availability logic lives.

\subsection{Three constraints and the choices they force}
\label{sec:choices}

\paragraph{A small team implies a vendor-supported availability layer.}
When the operating team is small, the most valuable property of a
component is not its feature set but the existence of someone else's
documentation, errata and support contract. On Red Hat Enterprise
Linux~9 and its derivatives that component is Pacemaker with
Corosync~\cite{pacemaker,corosync,rhelha}: shipped in a supported
channel, documented by the vendor, and with failure modes described in
material the administrator can reach without reading source code.
Maturity is precisely what a site with no platform team should be
buying, so we build on Pacemaker rather than introduce a second control
plane.

\paragraph{Containerised services imply a runtime that systemd -- and
therefore Pacemaker -- already understands.}
The services these sites run are increasingly distributed as containers,
so the availability layer must manage containers rather than packages.
Podman with Quadlet resolves this without an
adapter~\cite{podman,quadlet}: Quadlet translates declarative unit files
into systemd service units at generator time, after which a container is
an ordinary systemd service. Pacemaker manages systemd units natively,
so no container-specific resource agent, no daemon and no second
lifecycle manager is required.

\paragraph{No external shared storage implies replication between the
nodes themselves.}
A classical Pacemaker service assumes the data a failing-over service
needs is reachable from every node, typically on an NFS server or a SAN.
The sites we target do not have that, and adding it would introduce a
new single point of failure to be made highly available in turn. What
these services must carry across a failover is not bulk data but their
configuration and state -- the front-end's configuration tree, the
compute element's job spool, the accounting publisher's cached records:
a small, write-sensitive working set. DRBD~\cite{drbd9} replicates it
synchronously between the cluster nodes themselves and exposes a single
virtual block device, so the service finds an identical configuration
and state directory on whichever node currently runs it, with no storage
appliance involved.

A fourth characteristic shapes the networking rather than the
availability design. Data-intensive workloads are sensitive to the
overhead of container networking: a bridge-and-NAT path or a VXLAN
overlay adds per-packet work and an address space that does not exist
outside the host. With the in-kernel IPVLAN driver~\cite{ipvlan} in L2
mode a container attaches directly to a physical NIC and holds an
address on the physical subnet, so it is an ordinary host on the LAN as
far as clients, firewalls and monitoring are concerned, and it keeps
that address when restarted on another node.

\subsection{The remaining problem, and our contribution}

Each of the four components is individually documented and individually
comprehensible. The difficulty lies in the \emph{seams} between them. A
working three-node service requires a DRBD resource file, an LVM layout,
a Quadlet unit set, a promotable clone definition, a filesystem
resource, an ordering constraint, a colocation constraint with the
correct role on the correct side, a fencing device, and a firewall
policy identical on every node so that a failover does not silently lose
connectivity. Each is a small amount of text; keeping all of them
mutually consistent, and consistent after a change, is where the time
goes -- and it is exactly the work that does not survive being
interrupted.

Our contributions are: a detailed account of the DRBD-to-Pacemaker
promotion chain, including the definitions required for correct failover
and the specific ways in which plausible-looking definitions fail
silently (Sect.~\ref{sec:promote}); a generator-based tool that emits
that chain together with the rest of the stack, and the policy --
adopted during development -- that every change to cluster state is
expressed as a generated Ansible playbook rather than a direct command
(Sect.~\ref{sec:design}); a validation strategy in which generated
artefacts are checked by the real deployment tools with negative
controls, and the defects this uncovered that substring unit tests had
accepted (Sect.~\ref{sec:validation}); and experience from the
production deployment at the CMS Tier-2 site T2\_KR\_KISTI
(Sect.~\ref{sec:deployment}).

\section{The DRBD--Pacemaker promotion chain}
\label{sec:promote}

\subsection{What promotion has to guarantee}

For a containerised service whose configuration and state live on a
replicated device, a correct failover must satisfy four properties at
once:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Exclusivity.} At most one node holds the DRBD device in
      the primary role at any time. Two primaries with a mounted
      non-cluster filesystem means divergent state.
\item \textbf{Ordering.} The filesystem is mounted only after promotion
      completes, and the containers start only after the mount succeeds.
      On relocation the reverse must hold: containers stop, filesystem
      unmounts, device demotes.
\item \textbf{Co-location.} The mount and the containers run on the node
      holding the promoted instance, not merely on \emph{some} node.
\item \textbf{Observability of failure.} If promotion fails or the
      promoted instance becomes unhealthy, the cluster must take a
      defined action, and that action must be the one the administrator
      configured.
\end{enumerate}

Properties 1--3 are expressed with resource and constraint definitions;
property 4 depends on an interaction between the resource's
\texttt{on-fail} setting and cluster-wide fencing configuration, and is
the least obvious of the four.

\subsection{Primitive and promotable clone}

DRBD is registered with the LINBIT-provided OCF agent and then converted
into a promotable clone, as in Listing~\ref{lst:drbd}.

\texttt{promoted-max=1} with \texttt{promoted-node-max=1} enforces
exclusivity (property~1), and \texttt{notify=true} is required by the
DRBD agent, which uses clone notifications to coordinate role changes
among peers. The operation set is written out in full because the
agent's defaults differ from the values the DRBD documentation
recommends for a Pacemaker-managed resource, and a partially specified
set is indistinguishable, on inspection of the CIB, from a deliberately
chosen one.

\subsection{Role-scoped monitors and the interval collision}
\label{sec:monitor}

A promotable DRBD clone needs two recurring monitors, one per role,
because the health criteria differ: a promoted instance must be primary
and connected, an unpromoted instance must be secondary and in sync. The
obvious encoding is two monitors with the same interval and different
\texttt{role} attributes.

That encoding is rejected. \texttt{pcs} (observed with version 0.11.11)
refuses the resource creation with

\begin{footnotesize}
\begin{verbatim}
Error: multiple specification of the same
operation with the same interval:
monitor with intervals 20s, 20s
\end{verbatim}
\end{footnotesize}

\noindent
because the operation identity it constructs does not include the role.
An earlier version of our generator emitted \texttt{20s} for both, so
the generated script failed at its first resource creation and --
because it runs under \texttt{set -euo pipefail} -- stopped there,
leaving the cluster partially configured. The tool now emits distinct
intervals and, following LINBIT's own examples, chooses values that are
not multiples of one another (\texttt{29s} and \texttt{31s} by default)
so the two monitors do not repeatedly coincide. A regression test
asserts both that two role-scoped monitors are present and that their
default intervals are mutually non-divisible.

The placement of \texttt{on-fail} is a related subtlety. It is attached
to the \emph{promoted}-role monitor only, because the set of values the
interface offers (\texttt{fence}, \texttt{block}, \texttt{stop},
\texttt{ignore}, \texttt{demote}) is fully valid only there: Pacemaker
accepts \texttt{demote} only for the \texttt{promote} action and for
recurring monitors with \texttt{role=Promoted}. Attaching the same value
to the unpromoted monitor produces a definition that \texttt{pcs}
rejects for some user choices and accepts for others -- input-dependent
breakage, which is unpleasant to diagnose in the field.

\subsection{The ordering and colocation chain}

With the clone defined, the dependent resources are chained. For a
service with one DRBD volume, a filesystem and a container group, the
tool generates:

\begin{footnotesize}
\begin{verbatim}
pcs constraint order promote drbd-r0-clone \
    then start fs-r0 kind=Mandatory \
    id=ord-fs-r0-after-drbd-r0-clone
pcs constraint colocation add fs-r0 \
    with Promoted drbd-r0-clone \
    score=INFINITY \
    id=col-fs-r0-with-drbd-r0-clone
pcs constraint order start fs-r0 \
    then start grp-myapp kind=Mandatory \
    id=ord-grp-myapp-after-fs-r0
pcs constraint colocation add grp-myapp \
    with fs-r0 score=INFINITY \
    id=col-grp-myapp-with-fs-r0
\end{verbatim}
\end{footnotesize}

The ordering constraints give property~2 and the colocation constraints
property~3. Pacemaker derives the reverse order on relocation
automatically, so stop, unmount and demote occur in the correct sequence
without a second set of constraints. Two details of this chain were
wrong in earlier versions and are worth recording.

\paragraph{Role position in the colocation syntax.}
The role qualifier in \texttt{pcs constraint colocation add} belongs
\emph{before} the resource identifier it qualifies, i.e.\
\texttt{... with Promoted drbd-r0-clone}. Our generator originally
emitted \texttt{... with drbd-r0-clone role=Promoted}, which reads
naturally and which the command-line parser accepts as an option. The
resulting CIB update does not validate:

\begin{footnotesize}
\begin{verbatim}
Error: Unable to update cib
Update does not conform to the configured schema
\end{verbatim}
\end{footnotesize}

\noindent
Under \texttt{set -euo pipefail} this aborts the run. In an earlier,
non-guarded version the colocation was simply absent, and the filesystem
resource was then free to start on a node that did not hold the promoted
DRBD instance, where the mount failed. The visible symptom was a
filesystem resource failure several steps removed from the cause.

\paragraph{The group, not its members, is the colocation target.}
When a service groups a pod with its containers, the constraints must
attach to the group. The interface submits \texttt{after\_fs: ""} for a
DRBD volume with no filesystem; if the route handler does not normalise
that empty string to a null value, the generator's filter matches
neither the "has a filesystem" branch nor the "has none" branch, and
colocation with \texttt{INFINITY} is emitted against each group member
individually. Pacemaker then treats the members as independently placed
resources, defeating the purpose of the group. The normalisation now
happens in the route handler, and a test asserts that constraints never
name a group member directly.

\subsection{Clone count must follow the node count}

\texttt{clone-max} bounds the number of clone instances Pacemaker will
instantiate. With a value hardcoded to~2 -- adequate for the two-node
case the tool was first written for -- a three-node cluster never places
a DRBD instance on its third node. The cluster reports no error: the
configuration is valid, the service is available, and the third node is
simply never a failover candidate. The tool now derives
\texttt{clone-max} from the number of nodes selected in the cluster
section, and when the node list has not yet been filled in falls back to
three -- the default target size -- rather than to a value that would
silently exclude nodes.

\subsection{Fencing: two switches that must agree}

Property~4 depends on two independent settings that are easy to
configure inconsistently.

The first is the resource-level \texttt{on-fail} attribute. If
\texttt{on-fail=fence} is set while the cluster property
\texttt{stonith-enabled} is \texttt{false}, \texttt{pcs} accepts the
configuration and returns success; Pacemaker then downgrades the action
to \texttt{stop} at runtime, silently. The administrator has selected
fencing, no error is reported anywhere, and no fencing occurs. Because
the combination is realistic -- fencing is commonly disabled while a
test cluster is built -- the generator detects it and emits an explicit
warning block into the generated script naming the affected resources.
STONITH itself is configured as \texttt{fence\_ipmilan} devices, one per
node, which is what prevents the split-brain scenario that property~1
rules out at the resource level.

The second is DRBD's own fencing policy. Setting
\texttt{fencing resource-only} instructs DRBD to consult the cluster
before allowing promotion, through a \emph{fence-peer handler}. If the
handler is not configured, a promotion attempted while the peer is
unreachable does not fail quickly: it blocks until the Pacemaker
operation timeout expires and is then reported as a failure, which in
turn triggers the resource's failure handling. The generator therefore
deploys \texttt{crm-fence-peer.9.sh} in
\texttt{/etc/drbd.d/global\_common.conf} whenever \texttt{resource-only}
fencing is selected, and the interface states the dependency at the
point where the policy is chosen.

\subsection{Multiple volumes: colocation has a direction}

A service may depend on more than one DRBD volume, in which case the
volumes must be promoted on the same node. This requires a colocation
between two promotable clones, and the relation is \emph{directed}: one
clone is the anchor and the other follows it, as
\texttt{Promoted follower with Promoted anchor}. Generating it in the
opposite direction produces a configuration that looks symmetric and is
not -- an attempt to relocate only the follower is then blocked by a
\texttt{-INFINITY} promotion score, and the cluster refuses the move
with no obvious explanation. The interface presents this as an explicit
anchor/follower pair and labels which side to move, because the correct
choice is not recoverable from the generated constraint by inspection.

\subsection{Idempotency and teardown}

Two properties proved necessary once the tool was used on a cluster that
already existed. First, \emph{idempotency}: because the generated script
runs under \texttt{set -euo pipefail}, a \texttt{pcs resource create}
that fails with "already exists" stops the run after the preceding
commands have been applied, leaving a partially configured cluster and
an administrator who must determine by hand how far the script got.
Every resource creation is now wrapped in an existence check
(\texttt{pcs resource config <id>} returns non-zero when the resource is
absent), and every constraint creation in the corresponding check
against \texttt{pcs constraint --full}, so re-running on a configured
cluster is a no-op that reports what it skipped.

Second, \emph{teardown}. A generator that can only create is of limited
use when a configuration must be revised: the administrator has to
inspect the CIB and decide what to delete, which is the task the tool
exists to remove. The tool emits a matching teardown script that removes
only the objects it created, in reverse dependency order -- constraints,
groups, systemd and filesystem resources, the DRBD clone, then STONITH
devices. The order is mandatory, not cosmetic: a remaining constraint
blocks resource deletion and a remaining group blocks member deletion.
Resources are stopped normally rather than with \texttt{--force}, so the
filesystem unmounts and DRBD demotes cleanly. The script explicitly does
\emph{not} touch the DRBD volume or the \texttt{.res} file: it withdraws
the storage from Pacemaker management and leaves the data alone. Tests
assert the ordering, the re-runnability, and the absence of any
storage-destroying command.

\section{Generating the stack}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{A generator, not a controller}

The chain of Sect.~\ref{sec:promote} is what \emph{IPVLAN Container
Manager} produces. The central design decision is that the tool produces
text and then gets out of the way: there is no agent on the cluster
nodes, no reconciliation loop, and no runtime dependency on the tool
being available. A generator can be abandoned without stranding the
cluster, whereas a controller cannot; if the site later manages the
cluster by hand, the artefacts the tool produced are exactly the
artefacts a handwritten deployment would have contained. This is the
reasoning of Sect.~\ref{sec:choices} applied once more -- minimise the
number of components whose continued existence the site depends on.
Every artefact is emitted as a complete file including comments that
state \emph{why} a setting is present; for a two-person team, a
generated script whose comments record that \texttt{on-fail=fence} is
inert while \texttt{stonith-enabled=false} is worth more than a
graphical rendering of the same configuration.

The application is a single Rust binary (about 12\,000 lines of Rust and
7\,000 of HTML templates). Requests carry no server-side session state,
and each submission is an independent transformation from a set of
values to the set of files listed in Table~\ref{tab:artefacts}; a small
SQLite database holds only inventory that must persist across restarts.
At startup a background task scans \texttt{/etc/drbd.d/},
\texttt{/etc/containers/systemd/} and the local \texttt{pcsd} REST API,
so an administrator with a partially configured cluster does not start
from an empty form. Each generator is a pure function from a model
structure to a string, which is what makes the validation strategy of
Sect.~\ref{sec:validation} possible.

\subsection{Ansible as the only mutation path}

Early in development the tool was permitted to execute a few commands
directly on cluster nodes, for instance to inspect firewall state while
diagnosing a failover. This was removed. The rule adopted instead is
that any change to the state of a cluster node must be expressed as a
generated Ansible playbook~\cite{ansible} that the administrator can
read before it runs and re-run afterwards.

The reason is not purity. A direct command is invisible after the fact:
it appears in no artefact, it cannot be reviewed, and it cannot be
replayed on a node that was down at the time. At a minimally-staffed
site the last property matters most, because the person who applies a
change is frequently not the person who diagnoses its consequence three
weeks later. Two facilities follow. Interface discovery: rather than
asking which NIC carries a given subnet on each node, the tool generates
a playbook that runs \texttt{ip -j addr show} everywhere and matches
interfaces to the configured subnets. Deferred substitution: when a
network's parent interface is unknown at generation time the Quadlet
generator emits the placeholder
\texttt{Options=parent=\_\_PARENT\_IFACE\_\_}, and the accompanying
playbook inserts an \texttt{ip route show to match <subnet>} detection
task whose output replaces it at deployment time, so the generated unit
is correct on nodes with differing interface names.

\section{Validating generated artefacts}
\label{sec:validation}

The natural way to test a generator is to assert that its output
contains the expected substrings. We did this, and the tests passed
while the generated artefacts were being rejected by the tools that
consume them. The reason is structural: a substring assertion encodes
the same intent as the generator, written by the same author at the same
time. If the intent is wrong -- if the author believes that
\texttt{on-io-error passthrough} is a valid DRBD option -- the generator
and the test agree, and the test confirms the error.

We therefore added a permanent test module that submits generated
artefacts to the real tools: \texttt{drbdadm} for DRBD resource files,
\texttt{nft --check} for the firewall policy, the Podman Quadlet
generator for unit files, and \texttt{ansible-playbook --syntax-check}
with \texttt{ansible-inventory --graph} for playbooks and inventories.
Each validator is skipped, with a reported reason, when its binary is
absent, and a dedicated test reports which validators ran, so a silently
degraded suite cannot be mistaken for a passing one. Each check is
paired with a \emph{negative control}: a deliberately invalid artefact
of the same kind that the validator must reject. Without this, a
validator that has silently become a no-op -- through a changed flag, a
missing kernel module or a permission error -- would report success for
every input. The negative controls caught exactly that situation once
during development.

Table~\ref{tab:defects} lists the defects this uncovered that the
substring tests had accepted. Most are semantic rather than
typographical, and four of the seven would have manifested only during a
failover. A further defect in the firewall generator deserves mention
because our first diagnosis of it was wrong: two named sets generated
with colliding names do not produce a duplicate-element error from
\texttt{nft}; the rule set is accepted and the sets are \emph{merged}.
The consequence is a policy granting more access than was specified -- a
firewall that silently fails open. At the time of writing the suite
contains 79 tests, including the external validations.

\section{Production deployment}
\label{sec:deployment}

The stack is in production on a three-node AlmaLinux~9 cluster at
T2\_KR\_KISTI, the CMS Tier-2 site operated by KISTI, where it manages
the dCache~\cite{dcache} frontend, HTCondor-CE~\cite{htcondorce,condor}
and the site-BDII/APEL~\cite{apel} information and accounting services.
These three share the profile the design targets: each is a
single-instance, stateful service whose unavailability is immediately
visible to the collaboration, and each needs a modest configuration and
state directory rather than bulk storage -- exactly the working set DRBD
replication between the cluster nodes is suited to, and the reason no
NFS or SAN layer is involved. Each service is a Quadlet-defined
container group registered with Pacemaker as a systemd resource,
colocated with the filesystem resource carrying its configuration, which
in turn is colocated with the promoted DRBD instance, following the
chain of Sect.~\ref{sec:promote}. IPVLAN L2 networks attach the
containers directly to the nodes' physical interfaces, so each service
keeps a stable routable address across a failover.
Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the arrangement. All deployment is performed
with the generated playbooks; no configuration change is applied to a
node by hand.

demoted on the source, promoted on the target, filesystem mounted,
containers started. The constraint chain of Sect.~\ref{sec:promote} was
necessary and sufficient for this, and the failures described there were
all encountered on the way to it.

%% No network throughput measurement is needed: that IPVLAN is closer
%% to native than a bridge or VXLAN path is established knowledge.

Because IPVLAN places containers on the physical subnet, access control
cannot be delegated to a NAT boundary. The tool generates an nftables
\texttt{table netdev} ingress policy~\cite{nftables} with per-target
address sets, named subnet groups and per-service port sets. One
property of this artefact is specific to the HA setting: the policy must
be installed identically on \emph{every} node, not only the node
currently running the service, because after a failover the destination
address is served elsewhere and a node without the rule drops the
traffic. The generated playbook therefore targets the whole cluster
group. A related observation concerns \texttt{firewalld}: when zone
membership is determined by interface rather than by source address,
traffic from a peer whose address is not covered by the active zone's
sources is handled by a different zone than intended, and since
\texttt{AllowZoneDrifting} was removed in \texttt{firewalld}~1.0 the
previously forgiving behaviour is gone. Source matching was required for
the cluster peers to reach each other as intended.

DRBD can also be driven through LINSTOR~\cite{linstor}, which manages
the same replication layer from a central controller and writes the
resource files itself; the promotion chain of Sect.~\ref{sec:promote} is
unchanged by that choice, since Pacemaker still owns promotion through
\texttt{ocf:linbit:drbd}. We have recently added an experimental,
separate code path that generates a LINSTOR inventory and playbook, so
the approach is applicable to LINSTOR-managed volumes as well. The
production deployment described here does not use it.

\section{Discussion and conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

We have described the chain of Pacemaker definitions that binds DRBD
promotion to the start of containerised services, and the configurations
that every tool accepts and that are nevertheless wrong: monitor
operations whose role does not disambiguate their interval, a colocation
role qualifier in the option position, a clone count that silently
excludes nodes, a colocation between promotable clones generated in the
wrong direction, and a fencing action downgraded at runtime without
notice. What these share is that they are not detected at configuration
time, only at failover time -- which is the one moment at which a
minimally-staffed site has no capacity to debug. Encoding them once, in
a generator, is worth more than documenting them.

The most transferable result is arguably the testing discipline of
Sect.~\ref{sec:validation}. A configuration generator is a compiler
whose back end is someone else's parser; testing it against one's own
beliefs about that parser is not testing. The cost of running the real
validators in continuous integration was small; the cost of the defects
they found, had they reached a production failover, would not have been.

The design targets sites whose binding constraint is operator attention
rather than capacity: a handful of stateful services, three to seven
machines, one or two administrators with other duties, and no external
shared storage. A site that needs elasticity, dynamic scheduling or
multi-tenancy is better served by a general-purpose orchestrator. Two
limitations are worth stating. There is no drift detection: because the
tool generates and leaves, a configuration changed by hand is not
noticed until the next scan, and the scan is informational rather than
corrective. And two-node clusters have no natural majority; sites that
can afford a third machine, even one acting only as a quorum member,
should use it.

Finally, the choices of Sect.~\ref{sec:choices} were made with an
eventual migration in mind. Because the services are packaged as OCI
containers and described declaratively, the unit of deployment is
already the unit a container orchestrator would schedule; what is
specific to this stack is the availability and storage layer, not the
workload definition. We consider that claim provisional until
demonstrated rather than argued, and it -- together with quantitative
failover and networking measurements, and divergence reporting between
generated and live configuration -- is the direction of future work.
